Session 15 · Digital Identity · Why bother?

DID vs a normal database

Based on scenarios 1 and 2

Both scenarios could be built with ordinary databases, and today most of them are. This page compares the two approaches moment by moment, using the degree scenario (University, Student, Employer) and the KYC and card scenario (Bank A, Maria, Bank B). It ends with the cases where a normal database is the better choice.

Two separate questions

“Use DIDs” and “use a blockchain” are different decisions. You can have either one without the other, so keep them apart when you argue for or against.

Question 1 · the data model

Who holds the record, and who must be asked?

Database model: the university or bank keeps the record. Anyone who wants to check it has to ask that organization, every time.

DID model: the organization signs the record and hands it to the person. Anyone can check the signature without asking the organization.

Question 2 · the infrastructure

Where do the keys and revocation bits live?

A verifier still needs two facts: which keys does this issuer use? and is this credential revoked? Those can live on the issuer’s own web server (for example did:web), in a shared database run by a group of banks, or on a public blockchain, as in our did:ethr demo.

The one rule

What goes on chain, and what never does

The advantages above only hold if the chain stores lookups, never people. Both scenarios follow the same split.

On chain · public and permanent

Facts that verifiers need and nobody minds being public

  • Addresses of the university, Bank A and Maria: her DID is a random-looking number, not her name.
  • Keys and their expiry times: #controller, the phone key, the rotated key.
  • Revocation bits: bit 1648 for the degree, bit 5130 for the KYC, among thousands of others.
  • The history of changes, with block numbers.
Off chain · with the person or the issuer

Anything about a person

  • The credentials: Juan’s name and degree, Maria’s KYC result. In their wallets.
  • Passport scans and selfies: at Bank A only, which must keep them for the regulator.
  • Card number, amounts, merchants: between Maria and Bank B only.
  • Who checked whom: not recorded anywhere.
Be fair

When a normal database is the better choice

DIDs and a blockchain earn their cost when several organizations that do not fully trust each other must check the same claims, and the person should carry the proof. Without that, a database is simpler, cheaper and easier to fix.

Choose a database when

One organization, no outside verifiers

  • Only you read the data. A university’s internal grade book does not need to be verifiable by strangers.
  • The data must be edited or deleted. Personal records under privacy law belong in a database; a chain cannot erase.
  • You need many writes per second, such as every card payment itself. In scenario 2 only key changes and revocations touch the chain.
  • Users cannot manage keys. A database admin can reset a password; nobody can reset a lost recovery phrase.
  • A regulator requires a central operator who answers for the records.
The honest costs of DIDs

What the demos make look easier than it is

  • Adoption. A credential is only useful if verifiers accept it. Employers and banks must decide which issuers to trust.
  • Recovery. Maria’s offline recovery key is the weakest point: lost, the DID is frozen; stolen, the thief can add keys.
  • Standards still settling. Formats, selective disclosure and wallet support are improving, but not yet everywhere.
  • The issuer is still trusted. A signature proves Bank A said it, not that Bank A checked properly.
  • Payments today already have strong tools, such as tokens, 3-D Secure and passkeys. Scenario 2 is a model, not a replacement plan.
For class

Discussion questions

The employer never contacted the university. Who benefits from that, and could anyone lose from it?
Maria’s phone key is on chain but her name is not. Could someone still link her DID to her? How?
Bank A is hacked and its signing key stolen. Walk through what Bank A should do, using only the contract functions you have seen.
Which parts of scenario 2 would still work if the registry ran on Bank A’s own web server instead? Which would break?
Name one record at your own school or workplace that should stay in a normal database, and one that might benefit from being a credential.
A university closes. With today’s systems, how would a graduate prove their degree ten years later? With DIDs?
